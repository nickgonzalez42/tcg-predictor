What CardStock is, where its numbers come from, and how far you should trust them. Short answers first, with links if you want the long version.

What is CardStock?

CardStock is a free site that tracks what trading cards actually sell for and predicts where each card's price is headed over the next month, six months and year. We cover twelve games. Every prediction is saved the day we make it and checked against the real price once its date arrives, and the whole record is public, misses included.

Which games do you cover?

Pokémon, Magic: The Gathering, Yu-Gi-Oh!, One Piece, Lorcana, Digimon, Gundam, Star Wars Unlimited, Flesh and Blood, Sorcery, Riftbound and Cyberpunk. Each game has its own page of set price guides.

How do I find out what my card is worth?

Search for the card by name in the catalog and open its page. You'll see what a Near Mint copy sells for right now, what graded copies (Grade 7 up to PSA 10) sell for, a chart of its price history, and where we expect the price to go. If you know the set but not the card, the set price guides list every card in a set from the most valuable down. Start at the catalog or the set price guides.

Where do your prices come from?

Prices for raw (ungraded) cards are TCGplayer's market price, which is built from real completed sales, and we collect it every night. Prices for graded cards come from PriceCharting's sales history for each grade. A price only stays on the site while real sales stand behind it; when no source is trustworthy, the card shows no price at all. More in Where card prices actually come from.

How do you predict a card's price?

A forecasting model studies each card's own price history, how its set and its game have been trending, how many copies sell each week, and what kind of card it is (its rarity, its character, its artwork). From that it gives a price for one month, six months and one year ahead, with a low-to-high range around each one and a plain-English note on why. The About page walks through it.

How accurate are the forecasts?

Some land close and some miss badly, which is why we don't ask you to take our word for it. Every forecast is checked against the real price when its date arrives, and the results are published for every game and every timeframe: how far off we typically were, which way we leaned, and how often the price landed inside our range. See our track record and the weekly reports.

What does the low-to-high range on a forecast mean?

It's the span we expect the real price to land in about four times out of five. A wide range means we're unsure about that card; a narrow one means its price has been steady and predictable.

What is the difference between a raw price and a graded price?

A raw card is one that hasn't been sent to a grading company; its price here is for a Near Mint copy. A graded card has been sealed in a case and scored from 1 to 10 by a company such as PSA, and a high grade can be worth several times the raw price. More in How grading affects value, and the multiples by game are in PSA 10 vs raw.

Why does a card show no price, or an early estimate?

A card shows no price when there are no trustworthy sales to base one on, which happens with brand-new releases, tournament prizes and very rare cards. Cards from an upcoming or just-released set get a clearly labelled early estimate instead, based on how similar cards have priced at launch. Early estimates are rough, and we say so next to each one.

How often is the site updated?

Prices refresh every night, and the forecasts are updated as the new prices come in. A market report and a report on our own accuracy come out every Friday, and articles on individual games, sets and card types are published through the week.

Is CardStock free? Do I need an account?

Everything on the site is free to read with no account. A free account lets you keep a portfolio of the cards you own and a watchlist of the ones you're following, and see what they're worth over time.

Do you sell cards?

No. We don't buy or sell cards. Card pages link to TCGplayer, and those are affiliate links: if you buy through one, we may earn a commission at no cost to you.

Is this financial advice?

No. The prices are information and the forecasts are a model's estimates, and estimates are often wrong. What you buy, sell or keep is your call.
